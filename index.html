The Elliptical Grave

Chapter One

In the Beginning

The next great mutating of mankind is a little bit like the next massive earthquaking along the San Andreas fault: it is overdue, and it may happen at any time. — S.S. Eddington, Damocles Lectures

Professor Pioneer J. Reventlo delivers his annual "In the Beginning" lecture to the Prometheus Club at the West Science Students' Lounge. The lecture draws over a hundred attendees—unusual for a non-required, no-credit event—including many repeaters who return year after year despite (or because of) Reventlo's peculiar thesis.

Reventlo argues that humanity did not evolve from simplicity to complexity, but rather appeared suddenly "in high-styled and richly-gifted complexity" and has been declining ever since. He uses linguistic evidence: Chinese once had elaborate grammar that has eroded to monosyllables; Greek had eight hundred verb endings; primordial language had "more than fifty parts of speech" and "a common and popular vocabulary of several million words." The Cro-Magnon, he insists, was "king of the golden age"—taller, nobler, with bigger brains than modern humans.

The audience pushes back. Lucille Creighton, a professor of Historical Philology, admits all evidence supports Reventlo but argues they must reject his thesis anyway because "reason demands it." F. Cyclone Boniface notes that modern humans have actually surpassed Cro-Magnon in height and cranial capacity in recent decades. Adriana Thistle challenges Reventlo on artifacts: surely a hydrogen bomb is more sophisticated than a lance-head?

Through free association guided by Joseph Abramswell, Reventlo identifies his true goal: he wants to find and board a "time machine" that he believes exists as a natural feature—specifically, as an elliptical valley. Rosa Caprobianco recognizes the description: she was born in such a place, the "White Goat Illusion" valley in Calabria. The chapter ends with an assassination attempt: a fifty-kilogram lead weight falls from the catwalks, meant for Reventlo's skull but shattering only the artificial skull Catherine January made for him. Reventlo's "Cro-Magnon mind" warned him just in time.

Right Triptych
The Fall from Perfection

Reventlo's thesis—that humanity began in perfection and has declined—is a theological anthropology dressed in scientific language. This is the doctrine of the Fall: humanity created in the imago Dei, endowed with preternatural gifts, then diminished through sin. The "Cro-Magnon" functions here as an Adam figure, and the decline is a secular retelling of Genesis 3.

Notably, Lucille Creighton's response mirrors the modern intellectual's relationship to Christianity: she admits all evidence supports Reventlo's thesis but insists they must reject it because "reason demands it"—even while acknowledging that "'practical' does not have any meaning at all now."

The Prometheus Club

The club's motto—"Oh one more torch, and one still taller fire!"—invokes Prometheus, the Titan who stole fire from the gods for humanity. In Christian typology, Prometheus has been read both as a Christ-figure (suffering for humanity's benefit) and as a Luciferian rebel. The Prometheans seek humanity's "next great mutating"—a recovery of lost gifts that parallels both scientific aspiration and eschatological hope.

The Time Machine as Valley

Reventlo's free association produces "valleys" as the shape of time travel—specifically, elliptical valleys named for "games" or "illusions." This anticipates the novel's central conceit: that certain places are thin membranes between times, and that entering them is dangerous. The White Goat Illusion valley's name itself suggests unreality, play, and deception—the Greek ludi and agones that Reventlo invokes.

Chapter Two

The Golden Years Return

The World's great age begins anew,
The golden years return. — Shelley, Hellas

By morning, everyone has forgotten the significance of Reventlo's lecture—convinced by some force that the "Pioneer Reventlo Illusion" was meaningless. Yet three people—Arthur Ransom, Rosa Caprobianco, and Joseph Abramswell—decide to organize an expedition to the White Goat Valley anyway, treating it as a pleasant funded summer adventure even if they dismiss its deeper import.

Rosa reveals more about the White Goat Valley and its strange properties. She tells the legend of the San Carlo flautist who could blow notes that were preceded by their own resonances—effects that occurred before their causes. When he tried to withhold the note after its resonance had already sounded, "a great hand reached down" and forced him to play it, then broke his neck. The White Goat Valley has such notes of "preceding resonance," and people can be killed by them.

Meanwhile, five young Prometheans—Anabella Hilary, Raddigan Shrike, Dean Hayfield, Catherine January, and F. Cyclone Boniface—meet separately to discuss how to make the "mutational jump." Cyclone presents his theory of cheirognomy: that the lines of mummies' hands grow clearer the further back in time one goes, proving the original humans were more perfectly formed. Vivian and Curtis burst in, bleeding, claiming to possess all the evidence for Reventlo's thesis but being pursued by enemies. Then the Agassiz Memorial Chandelier—which something has been sawing through throughout their meeting—crashes down on their table. They escape only because Dean Hayfield recognizes the hypnotic influence trying to keep them seated.

The chapter ends with two revelations: the introduction of the Stoicheio (invisible beings who are "jealous of humans" and work to prevent humanity from finding its "hidden treasure"), and a meeting of three of them—1-212-1212, 12-121-212, and 12-12-12-12—who discuss how to stop the Reventlo group. The falling weight, they admit, is an old trick, but 12-12-12-12 will try it a third time. If that fails, they can always lead the expedition "stubbornly to go where we want them to go, and let them defiantly bully their way into the trap and the grave."

Literacy as Spiritual Impediment

Rosa's thesis that literacy inhibits prophetic function and should be "returned" in adulthood echoes certain mystical traditions. The "literacy nodule" that must be surgically removed recalls both the practice of trepanning and the broader theme of kenosis—the emptying out required for spiritual receptivity. The destruction of the Neanderthals through "always writing on the rocks" inverts the usual narrative of literacy as civilizational advance.

This also connects to Catholic mystical traditions of docta ignorantia (learned ignorance) and the via negativa—the idea that knowledge can obstruct direct encounter with the divine.

The Stoicheio as Demons

The Stoicheio function as demons in the Christian sense: invisible beings who possess humans, cause confusion, and work against humanity's salvation. Their jealousy of humans echoes the traditional account of Satan's fall through envy. The "hidden treasure to which humans hold the deed" is the beatific vision, the inheritance that the demons seek to prevent humanity from claiming.

Arpad Arutinov's treatise explicitly frames this as spiritual warfare: "Awake, awake, we are being robbed of our property and souls!"

Effects Before Causes

The legend of the flautist whose notes are preceded by their resonances introduces the novel's treatment of time as non-linear. This "defiance of all the laws of physics and cosmology and philosophy" anticipates the time stasis of the pavilion. The "great hand" that enforces causality when the flautist rebels suggests divine providence maintaining the order of creation—even paradoxical order.

Chapter Three

Whose Side Are the Goats On?

'The lines are drawn for the final battle, and only one question remains: whose side are the goats on?' — Karl Smelt, Armageddon is Imminent

Chapter Three presents the formal prospectus for the "Pioneer Reventlo Expedition and Excavation of Earth, Air, and Mind Sphere" as submitted to the university bursary board. The chapter is structured as a series of presentations by different expedition members, each making their case in their own distinctive voice—from Reventlo's visionary archaeology to Abramswell's "haunted air" methodology to Vivian Oldshoe's message written in persimmon jam on a priceless painting.

Arthur Ransom's prospectus openly admits they lack "strictly scientific evidence" but argues this is "one of the times when strict requirements be waived." Reventlo proposes excavating not just earth but air and "noosphere," digging out "the bones of the future as well as of past time." He mentions that Vivian and Curtis are being pursued to their deaths for guessing too close to the treasure. Abramswell confesses he hears voices from the "haunted air"—voices of persons dead for thousands of years, or perhaps unborn.

The most remarkable presentation is Vivian's "Handwriting on the Wall," written in jam over Groben's famous "White on White" painting. She claims to have flown "more than one hundred meters" that morning, and ominously notes that "the two bodies that will be discovered today" cannot really be hers and Curtis's. Jeffery Wind identifies one member of the bursary board as a Stoicheio named 12-121-212, warning that "we must move the Devil's Tail, for it is in the way where we intend to dig."

The seven board members—each colorfully described—unanimously pan the prospectus as "slush," "intellectually contemptible," and "the worst you have ever done, Arthur." Yet they approve it anyway: they need to spend money quickly to manufacture a funding crisis. The chapter ends with a skeptical commentary from H. H. Hughes of the Pentagram Group, who calls it a "Wild Goose Chase" and asks "What is wrong over there?"

External
The Treasure Buried in a Field

Ransom's plea—"There is treasure buried in this field"—directly quotes Matthew 13:44, Christ's parable of the kingdom of heaven. The expedition becomes a quest for the kingdom itself, the "pearl beyond price" that Ransom promises. The scientific prospectus is thus simultaneously a spiritual manifesto, and the board's approval (however cynical) becomes unwitting cooperation with providence.

Moving the Devil's Tail

Jeffery Wind's reference to Dante places the expedition in explicitly infernal geography. In the Inferno, Satan is frozen at the earth's center, and Dante and Virgil must climb down his body to emerge in the southern hemisphere. Wind's claim that "the Devil's Tail curls around under the hilly land of Calabria" suggests the expedition will literally disturb demonic territory—that the White Goat Valley sits atop or near the entrance to Hell.

The Original Language as Magic

Reventlo's claim that the original written language had "magical effect" and "word-charms that worked" echoes the Kabbalistic doctrine of the creative power of Hebrew letters. In this tradition, God created the world through language, and the original Adamic tongue retained some of that creative power. The "crumbling remains of old written Chaldee" that can still "transport and transmute physical objects" suggests fragments of Edenic power surviving in Semitic languages.

"Et in Calabria Ego"

Abramswell's phrase parodies "Et in Arcadia ego"—the famous memento mori inscription meaning "Even in Arcadia, I (Death) exist." But Abramswell reverses it: he is already present in Calabria through his voices, and death has no meaning for him. "I no longer believe in 'death'" places him outside the normal human condition, already participating in the resurrection life.

Chapter Four

We Are a Body. We Are a Banquet.

We are imprisoned by our enemies in a fortunate place.
We are a body. We are a banquet.
Now we open a transforming secret and feast upon it.
After much feasting, we will be quiet for a day and a night and a day.
Then the Spirit will come and move over us. — Catherine January, Transformation Poems

The expedition arrives in Calabria on the last day of May—though "the year, not then in doubt, is in doubt now because of a time anomaly." Fifteen people make the journey to Castello Capro in the Valley of the White Goat Illusion: the core team plus Leon Yuri, who is actually the Stoicheio 1-212-1212 in disguise. Some remember him being with them from Naples; others say he joined later.

The castle is perfect for their work: monks' cells for sleeping, great arcades for discussion, libraries, wine cellars. A ghostly servant named Perpetua brings them food—and Arthur Ransom, with his eye for financial advantage, immediately recognizes she is a ghost: "One does not pay for the service of one who is not in the state to be paid." Heavy equipment is lifted up the sheer cliff by a man and donkey using the "Forgotten Secret of the Ancients"—a telekinetic method that everyone understands while watching but immediately forgets.

The expedition discovers the pavilion, a pleasure-building that has stood "at least since Solomon was king" yet has "a temporary look about it." The majordomo Signor Vilicus warns them not to enter: "Good people do not go there. And such good people as do go there, they seldom come out alive." But they must visit it. Inside they find British and Scandinavian tourists, a weaving loom with a face emerging from the fabric, mummified effigies, and the "White Goat Document"—a covenant drawn up by the mummies when they were facing death.

The excavation begins in three strata: earth, "haunted air," and noosphere. Joseph Abramswell leads the haunted air work, summoning ghosts with electronic equipment. The most communicative is Cecelia Calca, a nineteenth-century ghost who explains the rules of her kind: ghosts sleep fifty years after death before walking; they can be from the past or future; future ghosts can be "obliterated completely" if details change in the present. Jeffery Wind ascends in a balloon to mine the noosphere, finding it begins just thirty meters up—the same height ghosts cannot exceed. Dorothy Blue-Ice asks the chapter's crucial question: "Is nobody alarmed that everything is going so easy? Does nobody of us know what an outrageous ambush and trap smells like?"

Chapter Six

The Trap Closes

'Let's talk of graves, of worms, of epitaphs.' — Shakespeare, King Richard II

The chapter opens with the ringing of the matin bell at the castle, now rung by Il Trol—the first single person strong enough to do so in a thousand years. The old castle lord, who had ordered the bell silenced because of a prophecy that he would die while it rang, has indeed died. The ghostly servant Perpetua announces this matter-of-factly, pleased that the gloom will lift.

The expedition members gather in a state of unusual euphoria. Evidence from their excavations pours forth in unprecedented quality—"absolutely 'golden' evidence" of a primordial magnificence. They speak of the "Eden Archetype," of the ordered rationality that was in the beginning, of the "haunted air" that is really "inhabited air." The euporic mint and the "well-being soil" affect them powerfully.

But this euphoria is a trap. Leon Yuri (1-212-1212), who has been among them all along, watches with his fingers crossed, praying in "gloopy fetish-prayers" that they won't escape. The expedition members, intoxicated by their discoveries, drift toward the pavilion—and someone shuts the door.

The "click" that sounds is barely perceptible, but several members instantly recognize its meaning: they are sealed inside a time stasis. The pavilion, which is itself a time machine, has been placed outside of time and space. There is no "outside" anymore. Dorothy Blue-Ice, Joseph Abramswell, and others explain the physics of their predicament: they are in a "stasis" where the entire cosmos has effectively disappeared from their perspective.

The chapter ends with an inventory of the twenty-one "Shouting Faces"—the members of the expedition as captured in a Scandinavian tourist's tapestry—and the grim realization that they may have walked willingly into their own tomb.

The question of whether a ghost priest can validly celebrate Mass raises the theological principle of ex opere operato—that sacraments derive their efficacy from the rite itself, not the minister's state. The Matin Bell as Eschatological Sign

The castle lord silenced the matin bell because he was told he would die while it rang. The matin bell traditionally calls the faithful to morning prayer, the first of the canonical hours. Its ringing here signals both an ending (the lord's death) and a beginning (the final act of the Promethean drama).

The Fall from Perfection

Reventlo's central thesis: humanity began in "golden wealth" with complete language, full powers, and magnificent technology, then declined into "bare poverty." This inverts evolutionary progressivism. Language had "more than a dozen pronoun and verb numbers... twenty moods and twelve aspects... with a common and popular vocabulary of several million words." Now we speak in broken shards.

Glossary of Terms

A guide to the specialized vocabulary, neologisms, and technical terms used in The Elliptical Grave. Entries are organized alphabetically within thematic categories.

Cro-Magnon Man

In Reventlo's thesis, the original and perfect form of humanity—"the king of the golden age that we have fallen from." Taller, larger-brained, possessed of full powers and complete language. The name derives from the Cro-Magnon rock shelter in France where such remains were first identified in 1868.

Djinn

In Islamic tradition, beings created from smokeless fire, capable of free will like humans. The Count of San Angelo is identified as a Djinn "escaped from a bottle" who won the White Goat Valley at berzirk during the Council of Trent.

Ghost (Period Ghost, Future Ghost, Restricted Ghost)

Cecelia Calca's taxonomy distinguishes several types. Period ghosts are resonances of the dead, recognizably dated by their appearance. Future ghosts are resonances of persons not yet born, who may be "obliterated completely" if probability shifts away from their existence. Restricted ghosts are "nailed" to a location by their own blood and cannot leave.

Lemures

In Roman religion, restless spirits of the dead, particularly malevolent or wandering ghosts. In the novel, used interchangeably with Stoicheio for the beings who oppose humanity. The Romans held the festival of Lemuria to appease them.

Prometheans

Members of the Prometheus Club, the academic society that sponsors floating lectures. Their motto: "Oh one more torch, and one still taller fire!" The expedition members are called "the Promethean company," suggesting their quest to steal fire from heaven—or to reclaim humanity's original flame.

Stoicheio (pl. Stoicheia)

Greek στοιχεῖα, meaning "elements" or "elemental spirits." In Pauline theology (Galatians 4:3, Colossians 2:8), the στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου are spiritual powers that enslaved humanity before Christ. In the novel, the Stoicheio are "crooked-winged caricature of angels who are jealous of the people," identified by numerical designations (1-212-1212, 12-121-212).

Troll

In Scandinavian folklore, a supernatural being dwelling in caves or under bridges. Il Trol is "an actual troll"—twice a man's breadth, with a skull too large to have been born of a human mother. His marriage to a "surface person" bridges the gap between species, "for the health and happiness of all the people."

Zombie

In Cecelia's taxonomy, a person "buried in the earth but not having the decency to die there." Unlike ghosts (who are dead), zombies are alive but have been underground too long. Nerone Estensi is identified as this type—literally dug up by Adriana Thistle.

Calabria

The "toe" of the Italian boot, a mountainous region with ancient Greek settlements. Rosa Caprobianco's homeland and the location of the White Goat Valley. Associated with mysteries: Dante placed the entrance to Hell nearby, and the region's air is described as "crooked."

Castello Capro

"Castle of the Goat"—the expedition's base in the White Goat Valley. Features monks' cells, arcades, wine cellars, and ghostly servants. The castle's majordomo Vilicus warns against the pavilion.

Cenacle

Latin cenaculum, an upper room. Specifically the room of the Last Supper (Mark 14:15) and of Pentecost (Acts 1:13). The ghost priest tells the trapped expedition: "You are not in a goat pen here. You are in a cenacle."

Elliptical Valley

Reventlo's intuition that time machines are not invented devices but natural formations—large, roughly elliptical valleys where "the demarcation between the several states of creatures is the thinnest." The White Goat Valley is such a place.

Noosphere

From Greek νοῦς (mind) + σφαῖρα (sphere). Term coined by Pierre Teilhard de Chardin and Vladimir Vernadsky for the sphere of human thought surrounding the earth. Jeffery Wind ascends in a balloon to "excavate" or "mine" this stratum of collective consciousness.

Pavilion

The ancient structure in the White Goat Valley that has stood "at least since Solomon was king" but always looks temporary. It is a time machine—"not properly a machine... rather a natural growth or a natural feature." Its hull matches the construction of Noah's Ark: "rosin-wood, tight with fibre, and covered with pitch inside and out."

White Goat Valley (Caprobianco)

The elliptical valley in Calabria where the expedition conducts its excavation. Named for the "White Goat Illusion"—a game, mirage, or dice throw. Rosa's family takes its name from this valley. Time moves more slowly here than elsewhere.

Analytical Projection

Joseph Abramswell's discipline—the study and practice of projecting mental images, ideas, or emotional states onto external reality or other minds. Doctor Fritz Otto's cinema projection is an extreme form of this ability.

Eden Archetype

Abramswell's crucial insight: "all archetypes are projections from the future and none of them from the past." The Golden Age is not behind us but ahead—Paradise is eschatological rather than primordial. Pioneer Reventlo was "inhabited a little by the 'Eden Archetype', a projection from the future."

Floating Constant (375 Days)

Jeffery Wind's term for a mathematically significant interval that appears throughout sacred history. It represents the period from the seventeenth day of the first month to the twenty-seventh day of the following year—the duration of Noah's Ark in the flood (Genesis 7:11, 8:14). Also the gestation period of the sperm whale.

Forgotten Secret of the Ancients

A telekinetic method of lifting heavy objects using "a man and a donkey." The technique is fully comprehensible while being demonstrated but cannot be retained in memory afterward. This exemplifies the novel's epistemological theme: ancient knowledge is accessible but cannot be held by modern minds.

Haunted Air

The stratum of atmosphere containing voices and resonances of the dead—and the unborn. Abramswell specializes in "haunted air excavation" using electronic equipment. He hears voices from persons "dead for thousands of years—or possibly not yet born."

Literacy Nodule

A hypothetical part of the brain that enables reading but blocks prophetic function. Rosa undergoes trepanning by Il Trol to remove hers: "Her literacy function is standing in the way." Those who remember the original language's magical power cannot read; those who can read have lost the power.

Mutation Jump

"The next great mutating of mankind"—a sudden transformation of the species comparable to the original appearance of Cro-Magnon man. All human change has been by sudden mutation, ritualized and staged. The expedition attempts to trigger the next one. "You can't make a mutation jump without breaking a few categories."

Time Stasis

A condition in which an object is placed "outside of time completely... into neutral gear timewise. And spacewise also, since they are interlocked." In stasis, the cosmos effectively disappears; "there really isn't any outside." The pavilion enters stasis when "the door clicks shut."

White Goat Illusion

The name of the valley, but also a dice throw, a game, a mirage. Il Trol: "It means that you don't have a chance. And then it means that a hundred to one against you will beat a thousand to one against someone else every time." Also called "The White Billygoat"—the ultimate long-shot throw.

Berzirk

An ancient game played on the Ark and at the Council of Trent. The Count of San Angelo won the White Goat Valley at berzirk, playing with "two future popes, two future Emperors." The game involves cosmic stakes—worlds and souls may be wagered.

Cheirognomy

The study of hand-lines for character reading (distinct from chiromancy, which predicts the future). F. Cyclone Boniface studies mummies' hand-lines, finding they grow clearer the further back in time one goes—evidence for Reventlo's thesis of decline from perfection.

Device Identifying

Jeffery Wind's specialty—classifying artifacts by their intended function through "extensive knowledge and sudden intuition." He can identify prehistoric bag-pipe keys, Model T Ford accessories, and elderberry presses by examination.

Floating Lectures

Weekly lectures sponsored by the Prometheus Club, intended to give "breadth and interchange and mutuality" to students of various sciences. Reventlo's "In the Beginning" lecture is the most attended, drawing over a hundred people despite—or because of—its heterodox content.

Trepanning

The ancient practice of drilling or scraping holes in the skull, one of the oldest surgical procedures known. Il Trol performs trepanning on Rosa using "ritual stone knives" to remove her "literacy nodule" and restore prophetic function.

Aorist

A Greek verb tense expressing simple past action without reference to duration or completion. Reventlo cites the First and Second Aorist as evidence of Greek's former complexity, along with "detached fossils of twelve further tenses drifting down the time stream."

Bioscopic Images

Moving pictures or cinema, particularly the "Selected Short Subjects" that Doctor Fritz Otto projects from his mind. Vivian and Curtis appear as bioscopic chase sequences, pursued by the Stoicheio across projected landscapes.

Ellipsis

A deficiency, something left out. The ellipse "also means a deficiency." The elliptical grave is both a shape and a lack—a vessel of transformation defined by what is missing from it.

Original Language

The primordial tongue spoken by humanity "in the beginning"—complete with "more than a dozen pronoun and verb numbers, twenty moods and twelve aspects, two score of tenses, six or seven voices, more than fifty parts of speech, a common and popular vocabulary of several million words." Modern languages are "broken shards" of this original.

Post-Alphabetical Form

A mode of thought or communication that preceded (and was superior to) alphabetic writing. Vivian and Curtis "think in post-alphabetical forms"—faster and more comprehensive than literate thought. Chinese retains "whispers of something older" in this form.

Cunae

Latin for "cradle" or "nest." The ghost priest uses it to describe the pavilion: "it isn't an unclean seal on the outside of this cunae. It is a holy seal." The grave/ark/cocoon is a cradle for rebirth.

Et in Calabria Ego

Abramswell's variation on "Et in Arcadia ego" ("I too am in Arcadia" or "Even in Arcadia, I [Death] am present"). His version—"I too am in Calabria"—suggests he is already present in the valley through the voices he hears in the haunted air.

Parousia

Greek παρουσία, meaning "presence" or "arrival." In Christian theology, the Second Coming of Christ. Jeffery Wind applies it to Reventlo's thesis: the evidence appearing "from the East even unto the West" is "the sign by which we may know the parousia, the appearance that was in the beginning."

Purgatory

Cecelia Calca: "Purgatory is not a place; it is a condition." The intermediate state where temporal punishment for sin is satisfied. Ghosts are "poor people in Purgatory"—not damned but not yet freed. The pavilion itself becomes a purgatorial space.

Scapegoat

From Leviticus 16: the goat sent into the wilderness bearing the sins of the people. Il Trol: "The reverse side of the white goat medallion shows the scapegoat, dead, dingy, unwhite." Vivian and Curtis are scapegoats—sacrificial figures who clear the way for others.

The Spirit Moves

The novel's final line—"Then the spirit came and moved over them"—echoes Genesis 1:2 ("And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters") and Acts 2 (Pentecost). Creation, resurrection, and transformation collapse into a single moment.

Apparatus

Projection & Media

A comprehensive catalog of all forms of representation, transmission, and mediation in The Elliptical Grave. The novel's plot centers on excavating buried information and projecting it into consciousness; accordingly, projection itself—mental, cinematic, and ontological—emerges as a central thematic concern. Doctor Fritz Otto's cinema, the bioscopic chase sequences, and the various inscribed artifacts together form a meditation on how reality is received, transmitted, and transformed.

A series of bioscopic (moving-picture) dream sequences experienced simultaneously by multiple receivers, depicting the acrobats Vivian Oldshoe and Curtis Bald being pursued to their deaths by the Stoicheio.

Nature of the Phenomenon

The chase sequences are not ordinary dreams but shared visions received by "many of our friends" through "bioscopic dream"—a form of telepathic cinema. Abramswell reports: "I see vivid bioscopic dreams of the young persons Vivian Oldshoe and Curtis Bald." The phenomenon spreads: "most of the alienists were getting the bioscopic dreams also."

The Chase Motif

"It was the old chase sequence, and these two persons, or 'ghost poems', were being pursued." The imagery combines acrobatic spectacle with eschatological dread: "These were eschatological chases, and the most likely eschatological alternative is always horrible death." The watchers feel "a growing fundamental death sickness."

Episodes & Transmission

The serial unfolds in discrete episodes over time. Vivian explains: "All our friends and interested persons can be getting these sequences by bioscopic dream." The presentation is described as a formal "PRESENTATION BY VIVIAN OLDSHOE AND CURTIS BALD, ACROBATS AND PSYCHICS." Curtis transmits secret information through "the skip-beats of his wounded heart" in "international code."

The Final Episode (Chapter 7)

"The final episode of the Vivian Oldshoe and Curtis Bald Flight Into Death serial was almost missed." Only Anabella is awake when Vivian bursts into the pavilion for her final appearance. Raddigan, half-awake, insists she is "only a cardboard poster"—yet Vivian interacts physically, tearing pieces off herself and eating them. The boundary between projection and reality dissolves.

During "Buried Alive," Raddigan Shrike's nails break from clawing at his projected coffin. Dorothy's Power to Break Projections

"Oh, I turned off the dark, Fritz," Dorothy speaks calmly after ending the "Buried Alive" sequence. Fritz asks: "Flesh of my flesh and blood of my blood, are you also the sharer of my new powers?" Dorothy can interrupt and terminate projections that have crossed into experiential reality—a power as significant as Fritz's ability to create them.

By the "Fountains of the Deep" sequence: "Doctor Fritz Otto was no longer the solo projectionist of these mysterious movies: Jeffery Wind, Dorothy Blue-Ice, and Raddigan Shrike had joined him in the composite projection." Joseph Abramswell's academic specialty and its implications for understanding how minds interact with reality in the novel.

Abramswell's Discipline

"Joseph Abramswell, the analytical projectionist." His field involves studying and practicing the projection of mental content onto external reality or other minds. He teaches "young mathematicians" and has been "manipulating" Reventlo for years through subtle projection—"a pleasurable hobby that I would never wish to terminate."

Reventlo Sustained by Projections

"Pioneer Reventlo was always sustained by projections that we didn't question too closely." When the pavilion enters stasis: "the projections that sustained him have been cut off." Dorothy explains: "To be sustained principally by exterior projections is to be a phenomenon rather than a person." Reventlo's impressive presence was partly a collaborative projection by those around him.

The Snake in the Skull (Chapter 1)

During Reventlo's lecture, "a snake had appeared in the skull" he holds—"quite a large snake that poked its head out of the eye-sockets." The narrator explains: "There were several students of projection psychology present, and snakes (being strong in the undermind) are among the easiest of all things to project. But which of them was doing it?" The snake is later revealed as hostile: it reacts "with lowering anger" when Reventlo describes Cro-Magnon man naming animals. An enemy is projecting against Reventlo even at the lecture.

Projection and the Eden Archetype

Jeffery Wind's dissection reveals: "Pioneer was a sort of ghost in that he had a very loose association between body and soul. He was inhabited a little by the 'Eden Archetype', a projection from the future." The Golden Age itself projects backwards into Reventlo, giving him his vision—but the vision's source is ahead, not behind. All archetypes are "projections from the future and none of them from the past."
